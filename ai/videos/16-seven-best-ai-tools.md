## What the video covers

Nate Herk (Herk from here on) lists the seven AI tools he says he uses every week to run his own AI-services business, after trying more than a hundred. In order: Claude Code and Codex together, OpenRouter (one account for many AI models), a rented cloud server from Hostinger, Grok Bot (cloud-based bots on a schedule; see summary 06), Glaido (voice dictation), an image and video generation service whose name the captions garble (probably kie.ai), and Trigger.dev (hosting for scheduled scripts). Most get one to two minutes: what it is, how he uses it and what a small business might do with it.

Three cautions. The title says seven, the description says six, and the video counts seven. The Hostinger section is paid for, and he says so aloud. He also says he is a partner in Glaido. He says the list is personal and may not suit other businesses, and he promotes a free starter kit for a personal AI operating system.

## How to apply it

Not a build. Steps 1, 2 and 4 follow his advice; steps 3 and 5 are our additions, not his:

1. List subscriptions you use for one feature and ask the agent for a small replacement, for your own use only.
2. Give each AI-powered automation its own key with the model supplier so cost per job is visible.
3. (Ours, prompted by a viewer comment.) Add a fallback model only after running the same real examples through both and comparing answers.
4. Split jobs into predictable ones (scheduled scripts) and judgement ones (agents).
5. (Ours.) Consider an always-on server only for something that must run when your computer is off.

## Relevance to Winstanley & Son

Account designs for AI cost and system watching are already under way (the CFO and CIO roles), so cost tracking is being handled there.

- **Cost per job.** The CIO and CFO designs track scheduled jobs and costs. The new idea is a separate key per job, so spend arrives already split, as a feed for those cost records. A model router is another company handling prompts, so patient data must never go through it.
- **Fallbacks need a test first.** The Make flows fail loudly by design, with a watchdog, and silent model fallback works against that. Any fallback model should replay saved fixtures in the test channel before it is trusted.
- **Predictable work as scripts, not agents.** Recall tagging, the daily checks and the supplier-invoice matching are already code or Make scenarios, with the model only explaining. This supports keeping that split. It adds nothing to build.
- **Voice dictation for staff and owner.** Typing long Slack messages and notes is where speaking helps. The reception agent already handles natural phrasing. Any tool would need a check on where audio and text go. An owner decision, not a build.
- **Do not copy:** a rented always-on server (Make and Mac schedules with a watchdog already run the estate), stock-trading agents, or image and video generation (real shop photography and the local reel builder already serve the brief).

## Account or role ideas this video suggests

- **Subscription Sweeper** [1:00]. Quarterly list of paid tools used for one feature, with the cheapest alternative, including whether a skill could do the job. Reports to the AI CFO, with the tooling register kept by the AI CIO.
- **Model Key Auditor** [2:20]. Checks each automation uses its own model key and cost label, and flags any unlabelled spend to the CFO and CIO. Reports to the AI CIO.
- **Fallback Replay Tester** [2:50]. May overlap any existing cost-and-model checks. Replays saved test examples through a proposed fallback or cheaper model and compares the category or wording, reporting differences before any switch. Reports to the AI CIO.
- **Agent or Script Triage** [8:38]. For each proposed new job, says whether a fixed script, a Make scenario or an agent is right. Reports to the owner via the AI CEO.
- **Dictation Safety Check** [6:28]. One-off review of any voice tool for where audio and text go. Reports to the AI CIO.
